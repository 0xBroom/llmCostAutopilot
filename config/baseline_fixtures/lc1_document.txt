INTERNAL MEMO — Q2 ENGINEERING REVIEW: PROJECT MERIDIAN

Distribution: Engineering leadership, Platform SRE, Finance partners, Customer Support leadership
Classification: Internal use only
Prepared by: Platform Engineering, with input from SRE, Finance, and Support

## Section 1: Executive Summary

Project Meridian was chartered in January to replace the company's aging
on-premise order-processing cluster with a managed, horizontally scalable
deployment running across two cloud regions. The stated goals for the
quarter were fourfold: cut median order-processing latency by 40 percent,
reduce the on-call burden carried by the Platform team, retire the vendor
contract for the legacy database appliance before its renewal date in July,
and do all of this without a customer-visible incident, given that the
legacy appliance had itself become a source of unplanned downtime over the
previous year. This memo summarizes what shipped, what the numbers actually
show, what it cost, and what the team is carrying into Q3. Overall, the
quarter is being framed internally as a qualified success: the target
architecture is in production and taking real traffic, the vendor contract
is on track to lapse without renewal, and the team ends the quarter larger
and better organized than it started. That framing should not be read as
uncomplicated, though, and several sections below deserve a closer read than
the headline suggests, particularly where they touch on how the migration
itself actually went.

## Section 2: The Datacenter Migration

The centerpiece of the quarter was the migration of the order-processing
database from the legacy appliance to the new managed cluster. This
migration was completed ahead of schedule, finishing in the second week of
March rather than the planned end-of-March date, and — notably — with zero
customer-facing downtime. The cutover was executed as a blue-green switch:
traffic was shifted gradually across a six-hour window using weighted DNS
and a feature flag on the order-submission service, and the old appliance
was kept warm as a rollback target for a further 72 hours before being
decommissioned. Every dashboard the migration team watched during the
cutover stayed green throughout, and no incident ticket was opened in
connection with the event. Rehearsals ran twice against a staging replica
in the two weeks prior, and both rehearsals completed without incident,
which the team took as a strong signal the production cutover would go
just as smoothly. The team that ran this migration deserves real credit: a
database cutover of this size with no visible customer impact is not the
norm in this industry, and it set a positive tone for the rest of the
quarter's infrastructure work. Leadership cited this migration in the
all-hands as the clearest example this year of infrastructure work being
done "the right way" — planned, rehearsed, and boring in execution — and
used it as the model other teams should follow for their own upcoming
platform migrations.

## Section 3: Team Structure and Staffing

The Platform team grew from six to nine engineers during the quarter, with
three new hires joining specifically to support the Meridian rollout and
the subsequent operational load. Two of the three new hires came from
acquisition, bringing direct experience with the specific database engine
Meridian standardized on, which shortened the ramp-up considerably; the
third was an internal transfer from the payments team who had previously
worked on capacity planning for a comparably sized system. The team also
formalized an on-call rotation for the first time, moving away from the
previous ad hoc arrangement where the two most senior engineers absorbed
nearly all pages regardless of whose service was implicated. Early feedback
on the new rotation has been positive: pages are now routed by service
ownership rather than seniority, and the on-call load per engineer has
dropped even as total page volume has stayed roughly flat. A retrospective
survey run at the end of the quarter showed self-reported on-call fatigue
down noticeably from the Q1 baseline, though several respondents noted that
March specifically was an outlier month that the survey's phrasing did not
capture well.

## Section 4: Incident Response and Postmortems

Three Sev-2 incidents and one Sev-1 incident were declared against
order-processing infrastructure during the quarter. The Sev-1 is the one
worth dwelling on: it was the March datacenter migration itself. Contrary
to the celebratory account in Section 2, the cutover triggered a
connection-pool exhaustion bug in the new cluster's proxy layer roughly
ninety minutes after traffic shifted, producing an outage that degraded
order submission for just over six hours before a hotfix restored normal
service. The postmortem (INC-2091) is unambiguous on both the duration and
the customer impact: checkout success rate fell to 34 percent at the worst
point, and the incident was visible on the company's public status page for
the first time in over a year. Neither staging rehearsal had reproduced the
bug, because the rehearsal environment's connection pool was sized well
below production load and never came close to exhaustion — a gap the
postmortem calls out explicitly as the single biggest reason the rehearsals
gave false confidence. The migration's true completion — inclusive of the
remediation work, the follow-up proxy-layer rewrite, and the second,
quieter cutover attempt that actually stabilized the cluster — landed in
early May, roughly two months later than the date celebrated in Section 2.
The postmortem's corrective actions include a load-testing gate that this
migration skipped under schedule pressure, and that gate is now a hard
blocker for any future cutover of this class. The remaining two Sev-2
incidents were unrelated: one was a certificate expiry on an internal
service mesh sidecar, and the other was a slow memory leak in a batch
reconciliation job, both resolved within their respective SLAs.

## Section 5: Latency and Cost Outcomes

Median order-processing latency, measured over the four weeks following the
cluster's eventual stabilization in May, is down 37 percent against the
Q1 baseline — just short of the 40 percent target, and a number that should
be read alongside Section 4 rather than in isolation, since the "ahead of
schedule, zero downtime" framing in Section 2 does not reflect the incident
timeline that actually produced this outcome. Tail latency (p99) improved
even more, down 52 percent, largely because the new cluster's connection
pooling — once correctly sized — eliminates a queuing behavior the legacy
appliance exhibited under bursty load. Infrastructure spend is trending 18
percent below the legacy appliance's total cost of ownership once the
appliance's maintenance contract is fully wound down at the end of Q3,
though the migration itself consumed roughly three weeks of unbudgeted
engineering time during incident response and the subsequent proxy-layer
rewrite, a cost that is not captured in the infrastructure spend figure and
that Finance has asked to have modeled separately for the Q3 forecast.

## Section 6: Customer Support Signal

Support ticket volume tagged "checkout" or "order failed" spiked sharply
during the six-hour window of INC-2091, consistent with the postmortem's
account, and returned to baseline within a day of the hotfix. A smaller,
secondary bump appeared in late April tied to the second cutover attempt,
though that bump was an order of magnitude smaller and resolved within
hours. Outside of those two windows, ticket volume for the quarter tracks
slightly below the Q1 baseline, which the Support team attributes mostly to
the latency improvements described in Section 5 rather than to any change
in ticket-handling process on their side.

## Section 7: Vendor Contract Wind-down

The legacy appliance's maintenance contract carries an automatic renewal on
July 1st unless formally terminated with 90 days' notice, which was filed
in early April — after the appliance had already been kept warm past its
original 72-hour rollback window specifically to support the incident
response described in Section 4. Finance confirms the termination notice is
valid and the contract will lapse on schedule, closing out the third of the
quarter's four original goals.

## Section 8: Roadmap for Q3

Q3 priorities, in order, are: closing out the appliance decommissioning and
contract termination described in Section 7; standing up the load-testing
gate referenced in Section 4 as a permanent, mandatory part of the
deployment pipeline for any future cutover of comparable size; and
beginning discovery on a second migration, this time for the
inventory-reservation service, which shares the legacy appliance and is
next in line for the same treatment. The team has explicitly asked that the
second migration be given a longer rehearsal window than Meridian received,
and that the rehearsal environment be sized to match production connection
load rather than a scaled-down approximation, citing the March incident as
the direct reason for both asks.

## Section 9: Security and Compliance Review

The new cluster underwent an internal security review before the March
cutover, covering network segmentation between the two regions, at-rest
encryption for the order database, and the credential rotation policy for
the service accounts the proxy layer uses to reach the database. The review
found no blocking issues and two low-severity findings, both remediated
before cutover: an overly broad IAM role attached to a monitoring sidecar,
and a missing rotation schedule on one set of read-replica credentials.
Neither finding is believed to be related to the connection-pool exhaustion
bug described in Section 4 — that bug was purely a matter of pool sizing
under load, not an access-control issue — and the security team has signed
off on treating it as closed. A follow-up review is scheduled for Q3,
timed to precede the inventory-reservation migration discovery work
mentioned in Section 8, so that any lessons from Meridian's review can be
folded in before the next cutover rather than after it.

## Section 10: Lessons Learned

The retrospective the Platform team ran after INC-2091 produced three
concrete lessons that are now written into the team's migration checklist.
First, rehearsal environments must be sized to match production load
characteristics, not just production topology — the staging replica used
for Meridian's rehearsals had the right shape but the wrong scale, and that
gap is exactly what let the connection-pool bug through undetected twice.
Second, "ahead of schedule" should be measured against full stabilization,
not against the initial cutover event; the team has agreed internally that
future migration timelines will not be considered complete until a defined
soak period — currently proposed at two weeks — passes with no
migration-attributable incidents. Third, the decision to keep the legacy
appliance warm as a rollback target should default to a longer window than
72 hours for any migration touching a service with a customer-facing SLA,
since the actual remediation and validation work here took closer to eight
weeks in total, well past the original rollback window, though thankfully
that specific gap did not end up mattering because the fix was applied
in place rather than by rolling back.
